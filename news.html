CRISIS MAGAZINE: A Leper’s Thanksgiving

THE CATHOLIC THING: The Virtue of Gratitude

NATIONALSHRINE.ORG: Ten Quotes About Gratitude from the Saints and Popes

BISHOP ROBERT BARRON:  Friends, today we celebrate the national feast of Thanksgiving. Biblical Christianity insists that our lives must be acts of praise and gratitude to the Creator.

Such obligations would indeed be burdensome and dehumanizing if they proceeded from a competitive and needy supreme being, but since they come from the one who cannot compete with us and who stands in need of nothing, they are in fact liberating. The gratitude that we offer to the true God is not absorbed by God but rather breaks against the rock of the divine self-sufficiency, redounding to our benefit.

In one of the prefaces to the Eucharistic Prayer in the Roman rite of the Mass, we find this remarkable observation directed toward God: “You have no need of our praise, yet our thanksgiving is itself your gift, since our praises add nothing to your greatness but profit us for salvation.”

It is precisely because God has no need of our praise that our act of gratitude is a gift; it is precisely because God’s plenitude cannot be increased that our prayer intensifies rather than compromises our participation in the loop of grace.

CATHOLIC DAILY REFLECTIONS: Gratitude that Heals – Your Redemption is at Hand

Though Thanksgiving Day in the United States is not an official religious memorial or feast, it is often observed by Catholics with a votive Mass offered in thanksgiving to God. As such, this civil holiday provides a meaningful opportunity to turn our hearts to the One who is most deserving of our gratitude.

The optional Gospel for today’s votive Mass presents two miracles. The first is the healing of ten lepers. In Jesus’ time, leprosy was incurable and highly contagious. Mosaic Law required lepers to live in isolation, cut off from the worshiping community and daily life.

Thus, as Jesus entered a village, the ten lepers “stood at a distance from Him and raised their voices, saying, ‘Jesus, Master! Have pity on us!’” What a perfect prayer! Their physical disease and isolation powerfully symbolize the separation and loneliness caused by sin. Sin is a spiritual affliction that distances us from God and His Church.

The first healing Christ desires in our lives is the grace to recognize sin’s destructive effects. We must feel its sting, experience the isolation it brings, and become aware of our desperate need for God’s mercy. Like the lepers, we are called to cry out with all sinners who recognize their need for mercy, “Jesus, Master! Have pity on us!”

Is this your daily prayer? At times, we grow so accustomed to sin that we no longer see its presence or power. Imagine, for example, that some lepers in that village had given up hope, ignored the news of the Messiah’s arrival, and failed to cry out. They would have missed the healing Christ freely offered. In the same way, unless we confront our sin and long for reconciliation with Christ and His Church, we may never cry out and thus never receive the healing offered in the Sacrament of Reconciliation.

Though the cleansing of the ten was a great gift, only one received something greater. This second healing lies at the heart of today’s Gospel and captures the spirit of Thanksgiving when celebrated with faith.

God’s graces must never be taken for granted. It’s easy to approach Him like entitled children, expecting care without recognizing the depth of His mercy. While God generously pours out forgiveness upon those who ask in faith, this is only the beginning. The journey that follows must be marked by a gratitude that overflows into worship and devotion. This is what we see in the one leper who returned: “And one of them, realizing he had been healed, returned, glorifying God in a loud voice; and he fell at the feet of Jesus and thanked Him.”
