BISHOP ROBERT BARRON

Friends, today we celebrate the national feast of Thanksgiving. Biblical Christianity insists that our lives must be acts of praise and gratitude to the Creator.

Such obligations would indeed be burdensome and dehumanizing if they proceeded from a competitive and needy supreme being, but since they come from the one who cannot compete with us and who stands in need of nothing, they are in fact liberating. The gratitude that we offer to the true God is not absorbed by God but rather breaks against the rock of the divine self-sufficiency, redounding to our benefit.

In one of the prefaces to the Eucharistic Prayer in the Roman rite of the Mass, we find this remarkable observation directed toward God: “You have no need of our praise, yet our thanksgiving is itself your gift, since our praises add nothing to your greatness but profit us for salvation.”

It is precisely because God has no need of our praise that our act of gratitude is a gift; it is precisely because God’s plenitude cannot be increased that our prayer intensifies rather than compromises our participation in the loop of grace.

Gratitude that Heals – Your Redemption is at Hand

Though Thanksgiving Day in the United States is not an official religious memorial or feast, it is often observed by Catholics with a votive Mass offered in thanksgiving to God. As such, this civil holiday provides a meaningful opportunity to turn our hearts to the One who is most deserving of our gratitude.

The optional Gospel for today’s votive Mass presents two miracles. The first is the healing of ten lepers. In Jesus’ time, leprosy was incurable and highly contagious. Mosaic Law required lepers to live in isolation, cut off from the worshiping community and daily life.

Thus, as Jesus entered a village, the ten lepers “stood at a distance from Him and raised their voices, saying, ‘Jesus, Master! Have pity on us!’” What a perfect prayer! Their physical disease and isolation powerfully symbolize the separation and loneliness caused by sin. Sin is a spiritual affliction that distances us from God and His Church.

The first healing Christ desires in our lives is the grace to recognize sin’s destructive effects. We must feel its sting, experience the isolation it brings, and become aware of our desperate need for God’s mercy. Like the lepers, we are called to cry out with all sinners who recognize their need for mercy, “Jesus, Master! Have pity on us!”

Is this your daily prayer? At times, we grow so accustomed to sin that we no longer see its presence or power. Imagine, for example, that some lepers in that village had given up hope, ignored the news of the Messiah’s arrival, and failed to cry out. They would have missed the healing Christ freely offered. In the same way, unless we confront our sin and long for reconciliation with Christ and His Church, we may never cry out and thus never receive the healing offered in the Sacrament of Reconciliation.

Though the cleansing of the ten was a great gift, only one received something greater. This second healing lies at the heart of today’s Gospel and captures the spirit of Thanksgiving when celebrated with faith.

God’s graces must never be taken for granted. It’s easy to approach Him like entitled children, expecting care without recognizing the depth of His mercy. While God generously pours out forgiveness upon those who ask in faith, this is only the beginning. The journey that follows must be marked by a gratitude that overflows into worship and devotion. This is what we see in the one leper who returned: “And one of them, realizing he had been healed, returned, glorifying God in a loud voice; and he fell at the feet of Jesus and thanked Him.”

A demon appeared at the Shrine of Padre Pio, in the presence of a cardinal, after the recitation of the Prayer to Saint Michael the Archangel

A 98-year-old cardinal who survived Albania’s communist persecution recited a 19th-century exorcism prayer in Latin at the shrine of Padre Pio in San Giovanni Rotondo on November 17, during a ceremony livestreamed by the Capuchin television network. Screams could be heard in the broadcast shortly after the prayer began, drawing attention to a rite rarely heard publicly in Catholic churches.

Cardinal Ernest Simoni, one of the oldest members of the College of Cardinals, recited the Exorcismus in Satanam et angelos apostaticos, a lengthy prayer ordered by Pope Leo XIII in 1890 as an exorcism against the action of the devil in the Church and the world. The prayer is distinct from the shorter invocation to St. Michael that Leo XIII introduced for recitation after Mass in 1886.

Before beginning, Simoni asked those present to pray for world peace, political leaders and Pope Leo XIV, describing the prayer as a powerful spiritual appeal against evil. The livestream captured cries and groans from inside the church. Some witnesses reportedly described people contorting themselves during the prayer.

The cardinal has spoken openly over the years about his ministry of exorcism, for which he has been authorized by the Church. He has said he continues to use the Latin version promulgated by Leo XIII rather than the revised rite, explaining that it is the prayer he has always used. The late Father Gabriele Amorth, the former chief exorcist of the Diocese of Rome, also preferred the older formula.

The prayer, however, is not intended for unrestricted use by the faithful. In 1985, the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, then headed by Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, instructed bishops to prevent laypeople from using the formula, particularly in liberation prayer groups where its use risked abuse and confusion with the Church’s formal ministry of exorcism. The distinction is important: Catholic Church recognizes spiritual warfare, but solemn exorcisms are subject to ecclesiastical authority and require appropriate authorization.

Pope Francis’ private diary describes a profound spiritual crisis and encounters with the devil

Pope Francis saw the “gates of hell” when he realized the depths of the clergy sexual abuse scandal and experienced mystical visions of the devil throughout his ministry, according to excerpts of his personal diary published Tuesday by the Vatican.

The most moving sections are Francis’ revelations of his own spiritual torments and what he described as encounters with the devil — visceral, tangible and seemingly terrifying visions. Two occurred in Buenos Aires, and the third after his disastrous 2018 trip to Chile.

“A few days after returning to Rome, I was in a meeting. I saw him and heard his voice, triumphant possessing everything. ‘Now I’ve gotten everything I wanted,’” Francis quoted the devil as saying in a Jan. 24, 2021, entry. “Just as I can’t forget his eyes from the previous two, in this case, I can’t forget the powerful raspiness of his voice.”

During the 2018 Chile trip, Francis created an uproar by discrediting victims of Chile’s most notorious abuser. After journalists, including from The Associated Press, challenged him during the in-flight news conference returning home, Francis commissioned an investigation and realized the victims were right, that he was wrong and had been misled by the Chilean hierarchy.

“Devastated because of the situation in Chile and the dialogue with the journalists on the airplane,” Francis would write later. “I became aware of how serious the problem was. … It was hard for me to admit what happened in Chile; I didn’t think it was so serious.”

Francis wrote on June 7, 2018, that in the Chile scandal, he “saw the entrance to hell. Pagan seduction in full force.”

As he dealt with the fallout in the next two months, he wrote: “What shame and evil this has brought on the church!” and “Once more the gates of hell. … May holiness and conversion abound in the midst of so many scandals.”

But Francis’ spiritual crisis wouldn’t come until 2021.

Cardinal Victor Manuel Fernández writes that the crisis was primarily related to Francis’ exercise of power, the temptation to omnipotence and belief that he was above the world. It’s a vice Francis admitted to publicly in 2013, in his first interview as pope, when he acknowledged a tendency to wield power in an “authoritarian” way.

It is significant that Francis was tormented by something his critics often faulted him for: an authoritarian, go-it-alone governing style in an absolute monarch and supreme leader of the world’s 1.4 billion Catholics.

For Francis, the temptation to omnipotence was the devil tempting him to “reductive curiosity,” or believing he alone could solve everyone’s problems, and essentially thinking of himself as God.

By Dec. 30, 2022, Francis had apparently overcome the crisis, by entrusting himself to God. Fernández writes that Francis realized that what began on Jan. 24, 2021, “was a powerful invitation from the Lord to become aware of an accumulation of interior darkness that he had to overcome so he could be free.”

CATHOLIC DAILY REFLECTIONS
Solemnity of Christ the King

The liturgical feast of Christ the King was added to the Church calendar in 1925, when Pope Pius XI instituted it with his encyclical Quas Primas. The eschatological event it celebrates has been in the mind and heart of the Blessed Trinity from before the beginning of time and was clearly proclaimed by Jesus in His public ministry. At the time Pope Pius XI instituted it, the world was in political and social chaos. After World War I ended in 1918, secularism began to grow worldwide, and communist and fascist ideologies emerged, leading many to question and even abandon their faith. The unity of faith and civil governance, which had been part of the make-up of Europe since the fourth century, slowly crumbled, and God’s sovereignty over the world was readily dismissed. Though the concept of the separation of the Church and State can be helpful to both the Church and the State, if God’s authority and laws are excluded from human governance, man is left to his own designs and inevitably goes astray.

Upon seeing the social and political culture of the 1920s devolve, Pope Pius XI felt that he needed to remind the Church and the world that there is only one King, and that King is not only the King of Catholics, He is the King of humanity, of the entire Universe. In his encyclical letter, Pope Pius quotes Pope Leo XIII who said in his encyclical on the Sacred Heart, Annum Sacrum, “[Christ’s] empire includes not only Catholic nations, not only baptized persons…but also all those who are outside the Christian faith; so that truly the whole of mankind is subject to the power of Jesus Christ.” Pope Pius XI goes on to say, “Nor is there any difference in this matter between the individual and the family or the State; for all men, whether collectively or individually, are under the dominion of Christ. In him is the salvation of the individual, in him is the salvation of society” (Quas Primas #18).

When Jesus walked the earth, He chose not to forcibly impose His divine authority and rule upon nations, even though many of His followers wanted Him to do so. Instead, He instituted the Kingdom of God as a spiritual reality in which we are all called to voluntarily participate, for now. Those who do participate are called to work to establish Christ’s Kingdom on earth, here and now, by governing according to the mind and will of God. The modern-day concept of the separation of Church and State is helpful insofar as the Church must be free from political interference and control. However, this separation should never lead to an exclusion of the King of the Universe from human governance. Rather, human beings must give themselves over to the control and dominion of the Great King, and then govern according to His mind and will. Pope Pius XI goes on to say, “When once men recognize, both in private and in public life, that Christ is King, society will at last receive the great blessings of real liberty, well-ordered discipline, peace and harmony. Our Lord’s regal office invests the human authority of princes and rulers with a religious significance; it ennobles the citizen’s duty of obedience” (#19).

Moving beyond the role Christ has in the governance of the Universe and humanity, this feast also reminds us that in order for Christ to truly reign as King, He must first govern each and every soul. Jesus is not only the Savior of mankind, He is also the model of Christian living. He Himself said, “I came down from heaven not to do my own will but the will of the one who sent me” (John 6:38). Just as the Son of God did His Father’s will while on the earth, so must we. Christ will reign as King of our lives only when we say those words with Him, by the power of grace that He infused into human nature. We cannot do our own will, but the will of the Father in Heaven. God’s governance of our lives requires complete obedience to His commands because His commands are perfect, true, and lead us to the fulfillment of human life. Only in Christ do we find peace, unity, harmony, and true purpose.

Furthermore, the ideal society will only become a reality if every individual is governed by the will of God. The more that happens, the more society as a whole will share in the Kingdom of God. The individual’s or society’s refusal to obey God will lead only to a participation in the kingdom of satan. Hence, we should see the institution of this feast in 1925 as both an invitation to share in God’s Kingdom on earth and as a warning that the secular, communist, and fascist ideologies that were emerging were leading the world toward satan’s kingdom. The same is true of our day and age in another way, especially as we see secularism growing.

The final and most glorious aspect of today’s feast points us to the end of time when Jesus, the great King, will return in all His splendor and glory to establish His unending Kingdom on earth, when He unites Heaven and earth into the new and resurrected Kingdom yet to come. On that day, Christ “will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead and his kingdom will have no end,” and we will all share in “the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come” (Nicene Creed). Sadly, this new and eternal Kingdom of Christ is often ignored.

Remembering and reflecting on Joseph Ratzinger’s Gospel of Love
BISHOP ROBERT BARRON
Daily Reflection

Friends, the story of Zacchaeus recounted in today’s Gospel is a particularly clear example of one of the most basic principles in the spiritual life—namely, that moral reform follows rather than precedes the arrival of grace.

Most of us are in the grip of what Thomas Merton called “the Promethean attitude.” This is the view that, just as Prometheus had to steal fire from the gods, we have to earn the divine love through the heroic living out of certain moral demands. But this is getting things precisely backward. God’s grace always comes first—and often unbidden and unexpected. Then it works—with the recipient’s cooperation—a thorough renewal.

Zacchaeus is described as a chief tax collector, which means he was a bad man indeed. He did not merit the inrushing of grace. But the Lord accepted Zacchaeus, even though he was unacceptable.

And from this invasion of grace came a moral reformation. The tax collector didn’t merit Jesus’s love with his display of moral excellence; rather, his display of moral excellence followed from Jesus’s unmerited love. To get this principle right is to get practically the whole of the spiritual life right.
DISCOVER MASS
Dependent on God

The new Pelagianism concerns the false idea that a person can earn their own salvation, metaphorically pulling themselves up by their own bootstraps. It denies that grace is necessary for sanctification and basically asserts that people are endowed with the ability to live a virtuous, sinless life, and they aren’t dependent upon God to live a life of holiness or to be saved. This also easily leads to a Phariseeism that says, “I live a virtuous life and obey the law, so if you aren’t doing that clearly you’re just more lazy or ignorant than I am.” However, like the Pelagianism of old, this new Pelagianism is a heresy.

So what does the Church teach about grace and salvation? Writing to Timothy, St. Paul says:

“He saved us and called us to a holy life, not according to our works but according to his own design and the grace bestowed on us in Christ Jesus before time began, but now made manifest through the appearance of our savior Christ Jesus, who destroyed death and brought life and immortality to light through the Gospel, for which I was appointed preacher and Apostle and teacher.”

The process of sanctification, that is, the process of God making us like himself, is entirely initiated by God. Salvation is a total gift that we do not deserve and cannot earn. Pope Francis puts it this way, “The Church has repeatedly taught that we are justified not by our own works or efforts, but by the grace of the Lord, who always takes the initiative” (GE 52). God always acts first. He acts and then invites us to respond. This is the story of Israel in the Old Testament, God freed them from slavery in Egypt first before inviting them to respond by following the Ten Commandments. He didn’t free his people because they deserved it.

However, as St. James says, “faith of itself, if it does not have works, is dead” (James 2:17). So we must actually respond to God in concrete ways, we must cooperate with grace. We cooperate with grace by freely loving others. Again, the pope says:

“Only on the basis of God’s gift, freely accepted and humbly received, can we cooperate by our own efforts in our progressive transformation. We must first belong to God, offering ourselves to him who was there first, and entrusting to him our abilities, our efforts, our struggle against evil and our creativity, so that his free gift may grow and develop within us….For that matter, the Church has always taught that charity alone makes growth in the life of grace possible, for ‘if I do not have love, I am nothing’” (GE 56).

Love, willing the good of someone else no matter what it may cost me, is the way we accept the gift of grace that God offers us. As St. Paul says, “For the whole law is fulfilled in one statement, namely, ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself’” (Gal 5:14). That is one of the sinister results of Pelagianism, a lack of love. For if I think that I have made myself holy than I build myself a little pedestal to stand on which I can look down and judge others from. Pope Francis is concerned with Pelagianism because it quickly makes us Pharisees instead of disciples.

The UN committee on women begged for money and at the same time faced sharp criticism for its longtime promotion of abortion. Even the Vatican, not a state party to the women’s rights convention, joined the criticism.

Quite remarkably, while the committee acknowledged that “motherhood is an important contribution” by women to families and societies, it said “many women are compelled into motherhood” by, among other things, the criminalization of abortion.

The delegate from the Holy See strongly objected to this. Acknowledging that the Holy See has not ratified CEDAW, but seeks to participate in a constructive spirit, “The expression abortion care is profoundly misleading, and it seeks to normalize the deliberate termination of innocent human life by presenting it as health care.”

“Such terminology cannot obscure the fundamental reality that abortion entails the destruction of an unborn human person, nor can it transform abortion into an internationally recognized human right,” the delegate continued.

The Holy See also questioned the committee chair’s assertion that the treaty is a “living instrument,” pointing out that this is inconsistent with the text of the treaty itself as well as the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties.

Other objections included the committee’s insistence that religious texts be interpreted in light of CEDAW and the recommendation that governments “implement programs on gender studies, and mandatory sexual and reproductive health and rights education at all levels of the education system.”

CATHOLIC DAILY REFLECTIONS
Thy Kingdom Come!

The Pharisees, like many Jews of their time, expected the Kingdom of God to arrive in a dramatic, visible, and political way. They awaited a Messiah from the line of King David who would deliver Israel from Roman rule, restore national independence, and establish a glorious earthly reign, marked by triumph over enemies. Their expectation was purely earthly, not spiritual. Thus, their question about when the Kingdom of God would come reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of God and His saving activity in the present moment.

Their question may have been more than simple curiosity. It was likely a test or even a trap. The Pharisees often questioned Jesus, not to learn from Him, but to expose Him as a fraud and undermine His credibility. Relying on human wisdom alone, they used that “wisdom” as a weapon to discredit our Lord. They may have hoped Jesus would recklessly offer a concrete timeline, giving them grounds to accuse Him of false prophecy or incite unrest among the people. How tragic that the greatest adversaries of the Messiah were the very religious leaders tasked with preparing His way.

Jesus corrects their misguided expectation of a political or visible messianic kingdom by revealing that the true Kingdom of God is not something to be “observed” with earthly eyes. It is not found “here” or “there” in a geographical or political sense. Instead, He says, “the Kingdom of God is among you.” The Greek phrase translated “among you” may also be rendered “within you,” indicating either that the Kingdom is in their midst—through Christ’s very presence—or within the hearts of those who believe.

Saint Augustine, in his commentary on the Our Father, teaches that the petition “Thy Kingdom come” refers to Christ Himself. Jesus is the Kingdom. Where He is present, the Kingdom has indeed come.

Moving forward two thousand years, if we ask our Lord when the Kingdom of God will come, He will first look into our hearts to discern the sincerity of our question. If He finds humility and a longing for truth, seeing that we seek not a sign but Himself, then He will reveal His presence to us in profound ways.

Do you perceive the presence of Christ in this world? By virtue of the Incarnation, the Son of God, our King and Messiah, remains present in countless ways. He is among us in the Sacraments, in His living Word, in His Church, and in the lives of those who serve Him in charity and truth. He is within us when we pray, surrender to Him, conform our will to His by grace, and reflect His love to others.

Yet often, as we journey through life, we live for tomorrow rather than for today. We think of when things will be better and overlook the sacred presence of Christ in the present moment. By learning from the poor example of the Pharisees, we are invited not to search for something distant or delayed, but to recognize Him who is already here, dwelling in our midst, awaiting our recognition, our love, and our witness.

Reflect today on whether you tend to seek God in some future moment or extraordinary sign, while overlooking His quiet and constant presence in your life right now. Ponder the ways He is “among you” and “within you” in prayer, in grace, in the Eucharist, and in the people He places before you. Ponder whether you truly believe that His Kingdom has already come through Christ and whether you live each day in communion with that Kingdom. As you reflect, renew your awareness that Christ is not distant. He is here. He reigns already in hidden glory, and He invites you to discover and serve Him with all your heart.

Midterm elections are federal elections held halfway through a president’s four-year term. Every seat in the House of Representatives is on the ballot, since representatives serve two-year terms, while only about a third of the Senate is, because senators serve staggered six-year terms. The president’s party almost always loses House seats in a midterm, but the president’s popularity matters: a study found that presidents with an approval rating below 50% saw their party lose an average of 37 seats during midterm elections from 1946 to 2018.

The Solemnity of All Saints and the pursuit of holiness
FATHER JEFFREY F. KIRBY
Saints are a ‘Cloud of Witnesses’ guiding us to union with God
EXCERPT HOMILY OF HIS HOLINESS BENEDICT XVI (1 November 2006)
Holy Mass on the Solemnity of All Saints

Our Eucharistic celebration began with the exhortation: “Let us all rejoice in the Lord”. The liturgy invites us to share in the heavenly jubilation of the Saints, to taste their joy. The Saints are not a small caste of chosen souls but an innumerable crowd to which the liturgy urges us to raise our eyes. This multitude not only includes the officially recognized Saints, but the baptized of every epoch and nation who sought to carry out the divine will faithfully and lovingly. We are unacquainted with the faces and even the names of many of them, but with the eyes of faith we see them shine in God’s firmament like glorious stars.

Today, the Church is celebrating her dignity as “Mother of the Saints, an image of the Eternal City” (A. Manzoni), and displays her beauty as the immaculate Bride of Christ, source and model of all holiness. She certainly does not lack contentious or even rebellious children, but it is in the Saints that she recognizes her characteristic features and precisely in them savours her deepest joy.

In the first reading, the author of the Book of Revelation describes them as “a great multitude which no man could number, from every nation, from all tribes and peoples and tongues” (Rv 7: 9).

This people includes the Saints of the Old Testament, starting with the righteous Abel and the faithful Patriarch, Abraham, those of the New Testament, the numerous early Christian Martyrs and the Blesseds and Saints of later centuries, to the witnesses of Christ in this epoch of ours.

They are all brought together by the common desire to incarnate the Gospel in their lives under the impulse of the Holy Spirit, the life-giving spirit of the People of God.

But “why should our praise and glorification, or even the celebration of this Solemnity, mean anything to the Saints?”. A famous homily of St Bernard for All Saints’ Day begins with this question. It could equally well be asked today. And the response the Saint offers us is also timely: “The Saints”, he says, “have no need of honour from us; neither does our devotion add the slightest thing to what is theirs.... But I tell you, when I think of them, I feel myself inflamed by a tremendous yearning” (Disc. 2, Opera Omnia Cisterc. 5, 364ff.).

This, then, is the meaning of today’s Solemnity: looking at the shining example of the Saints to reawaken within us the great longing to be like them; happy to live near God, in his light, in the great family of God’s friends. Being a Saint means living close to God, to live in his family. And this is the vocation of us all, vigorously reaffirmed by the Second Vatican Council and solemnly proposed today for our attention.

But how can we become holy, friends of God? We can first give a negative answer to this question: to be a Saint requires neither extraordinary actions or works nor the possession of exceptional charisms. Then comes the positive reply: it is necessary first of all to listen to Jesus and then to follow him without losing heart when faced by difficulties. “If anyone serves me”, he warns us, “he must follow me; and where I am, there shall my servant be also; if any one serves me, the Father will honour him” (Jn 12: 26).

Like the grain of wheat buried in the earth, those who trust him and love him sincerely accept dying to themselves. Indeed, he knows that whoever seeks to keep his life for himself loses it, and whoever gives himself, loses himself, and in this very way finds life (cf. Jn 12: 24-25).

The Church’s experience shows that every form of holiness, even if it follows different paths, always passes through the Way of the Cross, the way of self-denial. The Saints’ biographies describe men and women who, docile to the divine plan, sometimes faced unspeakable trials and suffering, persecution and martyrdom. They persevered in their commitment: “they... have come out of the great tribulation”, one reads in Revelation, “they have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb” (Rv 7: 14). Their names are written in the book of life (cf. Rv 20: 12) and Heaven is their eternal dwelling-place.

The example of the Saints encourages us to follow in their same footsteps and to experience the joy of those who trust in God, for the one true cause of sorrow and unhappiness for men and women is to live far from him.
